How long does it take to learn Italian?

A realistic answer for English speakers: how many hours each level needs, what you can do at each stage, and a weekly plan that works.

The short answer

For English speakers, Italian is one of the faster major languages to learn. The US Foreign Service Institute places it among the languages easiest for English speakers, at roughly 600 to 750 class hours to reach professional working proficiency. That is the top of the scale, though. You can hold simple, useful conversations much sooner.

Language learning bodies use "guided learning hours" to estimate each level of the European framework (CEFR). These are approximate and vary by person, but they give a useful map:

LevelWhat you can doApproximate hours
A1Introduce yourself, order, ask simple questions60 to 100
A2Handle everyday situations, talk about your routine and past180 to 200
B1Follow most conversations, tell stories, give opinions350 to 400
B2Speak fluently on many topics, work in Italian500 to 600
C1Express yourself precisely, with nuance and idioms700 to 800

Hours are indicative estimates for the whole learning journey, including study outside lessons. Your own pace depends on your goals, your habits and how much you speak.

What this means in months

If you have one 60-minute lesson a week and study about 20 minutes on most days, you spend roughly 3 to 4 hours a week on Italian. That is about 150 to 200 hours a year. In practice:

  • First 3 months: basic phrases, the present tense, simple conversations about yourself.
  • 6 to 12 months: everyday situations, the past tenses, comfortable travel Italian (around A2).
  • 1.5 to 2.5 years: real conversations, films with fewer subtitles, work emails (around B1 to B2).

Two lessons a week, or living in an Italian-speaking environment, can shorten this a lot.

What speeds you up

  1. Speak from day one. Understanding comes from using the language, not only from reading rules.
  2. Get corrected. A teacher stops mistakes becoming habits, especially with pronunciation and verb endings.
  3. Little and often. Fifteen minutes a day beats a three-hour session once a week.
  4. Learn what you need. Choose topics that match your life: work, travel, family.
  5. Review. Revisit new words after a day, a week and a month.

A simple weekly plan

  • One lesson a week, with a teacher: online or in Prague.
  • Five short sessions of 15 to 20 minutes: homework, vocabulary, one short podcast or video.
  • One "fun" hour: a film, a song or a recipe in Italian.
